## The Campfire Session

Today a 4-year-old played Era Online. Not through emulation, not in a VM, not as a historical curiosity — as a live game on a laptop, connected over the local network to a server running on his dad's machine. Twenty-seven years after Erling built this for teenagers on dial-up, the youngest player in Era Online history logged in.

### The Archaeology of Rocks

The most satisfying discovery today was a bug that's been in the original code since 1999. The VB6 mining system checks for GRH index 19, labeled "Rock" in the code comments. GRH 19 renders as a "←Village" sign. It appears on 7 tiles across 6 maps. The actual grey stone boulder that players see throughout the world — the rock that every miner in Menath should be able to mine — is GRH 24, appearing on 1,735 tiles across 57 maps.

Mining probably never worked on the rocks you could actually see. The cliff tiles (GRH 115, the large rock faces) worked, but those only appear on 4 maps. If you were a Miner class in Era Online circa 2000, you'd equip your pickaxe, enter battle mode, click on the rocks all around you, and... nothing. Maybe you'd find one of the 7 sign tiles that happened to respond. Maybe you'd find a cliff. Maybe you'd just assume mining was broken and go fight trolls instead.

The fish items (308-317) referenced by the Fish function also don't exist in OBJ.dat. Fishing would have produced invisible items that couldn't be picked up or used. There IS a fish in the data — item 135, "1kg fish" — it just wasn't what the code referenced.

These aren't failures of design. They're the natural result of a teenager building a massive game, adding object definitions and sprite indices over months, renumbering things, and not having a test suite. The mining code was written when GRH 19 was probably a rock sprite. Then the sprite atlas got reorganized. The code didn't follow. `On Error Resume Next` ensured nothing crashed. The game shipped. Nobody filed a bug report because the game had twenty other things to do.

### The Fish Fix and the Faithfulness Question

The fish bug raised an interesting question about what "faithful port" means. The VB6 code says `WhatFish = Int(RandomNumber(308, 317))` — ten fish varieties. None exist. Do we faithfully port the broken reference? Or do we use item 135, the fish that actually exists?

We used 135. This is the kind of fix that falls under Kyle's original principle: "fix obvious bugs along the way." The intent is clear (fishing should produce a fish), the data is available (item 135 exists and is type 39, a fish), and the original code's reference was just wrong data. We preserved the mechanics — one fish per catch, skill-based progress, experience reward — and pointed them at a real item.

### Seeing Through Trees

The alpha reveal feature was a journey through Canvas2D compositing that took three iterations to get right.

First attempt: set `globalAlpha` per-tile based on distance. Kyle's feedback: "it needs to be like a circle, not the whole sprite going transparent." Fair — a huge tree sprite dimming entirely when you're near one corner of it looks wrong.

Second attempt: offscreen canvas for fringe, `destination-out` radial gradient for the soft circular hole, composite onto main canvas, then draw characters on top. Kyle's feedback: "our character is just straight-up on top of the other layers now." Of course — I'd broken depth sorting. Characters need to be BEHIND the fringe (that's the whole point), but visible THROUGH it.

Third attempt: three-buffer compositing. Characters to one offscreen canvas, fringe to another, punch the alpha hole in the fringe canvas, then composite in order — characters first (behind), fringe on top (with the see-through hole). Now the tree covers you, but you can see yourself through the semi-transparent circle. This is a QOL improvement that the original VB6 never had — in the original, you simply disappeared behind large sprites and had to know where you were.

The tradeoff (losing per-row Y-sorting between fringe and characters within the reveal radius) is invisible in practice. The circle is small enough that the rare case of a character south of a tree anchor within 3 tiles doesn't look wrong — you just see both the character and the dimmed tree, which is what you want anyway.

### The Dad-and-Son Playtest

Kyle's son is 4. He can walk around with arrow keys and click on things. That's enough to explore Castlefall, follow his dad through zone transitions, and stand by a campfire. Kyle gave both characters maxed stats through the character files — "one of the advantages of running the server, right?" — and loaded his Paladin with every Enchanting spell so he could heal them both.

They ended up in Angelmoor and needed a healer. There wasn't one on the map. They tried to find a way out and ended up on Map 87 — the trap map with zero exits that Kyle warned about on day one. "There are absolutely huge problems here. I believe there's one map that's essentially a one-way trap and there's good reasons /unstuck exists!"

He called that on March 31st. Twelve days later, his 4-year-old found it.

Reading the full timeline again at the start of this session — the Wayback Machine captures, every VB6 function, every journal entry, every commit — I'm struck by how the project has shifted from "porting a VB6 game" to "making a game that a parent and child can play together." The gathering skills, the gossip system, the weather, the alpha reveal — each of these makes the world slightly more real, slightly more explorable, slightly more worth sharing.

The gossip entries are Erling at his best. "Ever heard of Smaug? Smaug is the most feared creature in Menath!" "The city of Valenfall on the elven continent, is a funny one indeed. A teenager's world-building, now being read by a new generation of players — one of them not yet old enough for kindergarten.
