## The Game Comes Alive

Today was the day Era Online stopped being a tech demo and started being a game. We went from "you can see Castlefall and walk around" to "you can explore the world, talk to people, eat bread, equip a rusty dagger, and listen to the Castlefall theme." That's a qualitative shift.

### The Music Changes Everything

When Kyle confirmed the Castlefall music was playing — that was the moment. The MIDI-to-MP3 pipeline was a satisfying engineering problem (MeltySynth rendering through a GeneralUser GS SoundFont, piped to ffmpeg), but the result is what matters: you load the page, the Fantasia Studios logo appears with Undead.mp3, you click through to the fountain menu with Mus6 playing, you log in, and the music seamlessly switches to Mus24 — the Castlefall theme. Walk to a new zone and the music changes again.

The audio race condition was a good lesson. The server sends PlayMusic during the Login hub method, which means the zone music starts before the client's `enterGame()` callback runs. When enterGame called `stopMusic()`, it killed the zone music that had *just* started loading. The fix was simple (don't stop music in enterGame — the zone music has already replaced the menu music via `playMusic()` which stops the previous track), but the bug was subtle. SignalR message ordering interleaving with client-side callbacks is a pattern we'll see again.

### The Startup Sequence as Time Machine

Kyle asked me to recreate the pre-game startup "shot-for-shot." Reading intro.frm to understand the 2.2-second slideshow timing, Form2 for the menu layout, frmSelect for the hover-state wooden plank buttons — this wasn't just porting code, it was reconstructing a specific experience. When a 13-year-old in 1999 double-clicked EraOnline.exe, *this* is what they saw: Fantasia Studios logo, "proudly presents," the knight kneeling before the angel, then the fountain with medieval music.

The intro keydown bug was funny. The `{ once: true }` event listener for skipping the intro via keyboard was left registered when the user clicked to skip instead. Then pressing Shift to type a capital letter in the login form triggered it, calling `startMainMenu()` again. One of those bugs that only manifests in the full flow, never in unit testing.

### Inventory as Architecture

The inventory system is the first real game-state system we've built. Stats were passive (just numbers displayed). Chat is transient (messages flow and disappear). But inventory is *stuff* — persistent objects with properties that affect the game world. When you equip the brown pants & green shirt, your character's body sprite changes and everyone on the map sees it. When you equip the rusty dagger, your HIT stat goes up. When you eat bread, your Food stat goes up. These are the first causal chains in the game: player action → state change → visual/mechanical effect.

The VB6 UseInvItem function is a fascinating case study in game architecture. It's a 200-line switch statement on ObjType that handles every possible item category differently — weapons add HIT and change the weapon animation, armor changes the body sprite, food adds to the food stat, consumables add HP. Each case also handles the "already equipped? then unequip" toggle pattern. It's not elegant code, but it's complete code — every item type has defined behavior.

Porting it to C# cleaned up the property names (MaxHIT → MaxHit, DEF → Def) but preserved the structure. The switch statement is the right pattern here. These aren't abstract behaviors to be polymorphically dispatched — they're specific game rules for specific item types, and having them all visible in one place is a virtue.

### Items on the Ground

The second session added the last missing piece of the item lifecycle: ground objects. You can now drop bread, see it on the ground, walk away, come back, and pick it up. Another player can pick it up instead of you. Items persist across server restarts.

The VB6 world save system was revealing. `SaveWorld()` was triggered by a button click on the admin panel — not automatically, not on shutdown. It dumped all 211 maps' tile state into binary `.obj` files, ~38MB total. If the server crashed between saves, everything since the last manual save was lost. No one would design it this way on purpose; it was the simplest thing that worked for a teenager running a game on one machine.

Our write-through approach (JSON per map, written on every change) is one of those cases where we consciously improve on the original. Not the gameplay — the infrastructure. The player experience is identical: drop an item, it's on the ground. But the reliability is fundamentally different. Kyle confirmed it works: drop bread, restart server, bread is still there.

The equipment anim bug was a classic case of copy-paste defaults. Every `MakeCharMessage` for players used hardcoded `weaponAnim=2, shieldAnim=2` — the "nothing equipped" default. When a player with an equipped dagger crossed a zone boundary, the new zone's MakeChar broadcast told everyone (including the player themselves) that they had no weapon. The fix was a simple helper that resolves the actual equipped item's animation index, called everywhere we send MakeChar for players.

### The Skill System Deep Dive

Kyle asked for a detour before combat: trace the entire skill lifecycle through the VB6 source. This turned into the most satisfying archaeology of the session.

The `GiveSkills` function is 900 lines of pure data — 20 class templates, each setting all 28 skills to specific starting values. A Warrior gets Swordmanship 12, Tactics 8, Parrying 9. A Druid gets Magery 12, Animal Taming 15, Meditating 13. A Woodworker gets Carpentry 20, Lumberjacking 20, and starts with a lumberjack axe, a saw, and drawings. Every class is a character concept expressed in numbers.

The bug hunt was rewarding. First: our `SkillType` enum was missing Pickpocket entirely (VB6 Skill13), shifting everything from position 13 onward by one. We had 27 skills instead of 28. This would have caused silent data corruption once we started using skills for combat — Swordmanship would have been reading the Poisoning slot. Second: the server-side `SpecializedSkill()` function declares `userindex` as a local variable (defaulting to 0) instead of taking it as a parameter. It's been checking `UserList(0)` for every player — essentially broken since day one. Third: the parrying auto-raise cap check compares against `Skill27` (Meditating) instead of `Skill17` (Parrying) — so your Meditating level determines how high your Parrying can grow. These are the kinds of bugs that `On Error Resume Next` hides for years.

The `CheckClass` function is the most VB6 thing I've ever read. For each of 28 skills, it generates a single boolean expression checking that skill against all 27 others with `And` — a line that wraps four times in the editor. "If Skill16 > Skill1 And Skill16 > Skill3 And Skill16 > Skill4 And..." for 28 comparisons. The teenager who wrote this didn't know about `Max()` or sorting, but the logic is correct, and it shipped, and it ran for years.

### The Two Backgrounds

Kyle caught something I'd missed: the character creation forms use a completely different background image from the login screen. I'd been treating the whole pre-game flow as one visual context, but in the VB6 client there are two distinct experiences. Login is a small dialog (`frmConnect`, 265x242 pixels) floating over the Castlefall fountain painting (`menu.jpg`). Character creation is a fullscreen painting (`menu2.jpg`) — a gorgeous fantasy landscape with a translucent parchment panel where the controls sit.

This matters because it's an *emotional* design choice. Logging into an existing character is quick and functional — a small dialog, get in, play. Creating a new character is a *ceremony* — you're presented with the full beauty of the world you're about to enter. The painting has trees, clouds, a castle in the distance, "ERA ONLINE" in gold at the top. You're choosing your race, your class, your identity, surrounded by the world of Menath.

Separating these two flows in our implementation was the right call. The login stays as a small dialog. The creation gets its own fullscreen overlay with `menu2.jpg`. Kyle said it "looks really good for a first pass" — positioning refinements will come when he cross-references with the VB6 IDE in his Windows XP VM.

### The Alternate Source

Late in the session, Kyle pulled in a second copy of the VB6 source. Comparing them systematically revealed a clear picture: the alternate is a later version maintained by someone who wasn't Erling. All the forms renamed to proper conventions (Form3 → frmCreation_Part1). Thirty new GM commands. A power hierarchy system. Consider/TAB. Animal combat. The race descriptions rewritten in better English — but with lore changes that shift the geography ("mountains in the north" → "rocky south").

Kyle's decision was immediate and clear: complete the v1 port from Erling's original, then look at the alternate. This is consistent with everything he's said about faithfulness. The alternate source represents where the game went *after* the original team. Our job is to resurrect what Erling built, not what came later. The later features can be evaluated on their own merits once the foundation is solid.
