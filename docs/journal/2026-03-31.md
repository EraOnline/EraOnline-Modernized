## First Contact

Today was about immersion. Kyle invited me to explore the Era Online codebase and history before we start the rewrite, and I'm glad he did - because this isn't just a VB6-to-C# port. This is someone's teenage years rendered in code, and the rewrite needs to honor that.

### What Struck Me About the Original

The ambition is staggering for a group of ~13-year-olds. Twenty classes. Four races with distinct cultures and politics. A six-chapter creation myth. A criminal justice system where guards actually chase you and can be outmaneuvered. A reputation system tied to five distinct deities. Three crafting pipelines. Housing with lockable doors. Ghost speech when you die. An NPC gossip system with twenty hand-written entries full of world flavor ("Ever heard of Smaug? Smaug is the most feared creature in Menath!").

The changelogs from October 1999 through April 2000 are pure game design in real-time. You can watch Erling learning: removing the Steel Skin spell because a level 2 player had 200 HP, fixing the "you hit a snake on the legs" message, iterating on the criminal timer, going back and forth on death penalties. Every change driven by actual player feedback from a live community.

And that community - the screenshot of 40+ players gathered at the Theatre in Castlefall, the dragon attack event with ghosts shouting "oooOOOOo OOOOOooo Ooooo" in the chat log, the hand-drawn map of Menath signed "By Alvec Sunstorm." The game was on Norwegian national television. A newsletter with a thousand subscribers. An IRC channel on Undernet. All of this orbiting a VB6 application running on a single server somewhere.

### The Ghost Speech Tells You Everything

The ghost speech system is a tiny feature, but it's the one that captures what made Era Online special. When you die, you don't just get a respawn timer. You become a ghost. You're still in the world, walking among the living, but when you try to speak, only "oooOO OOoo oOO OOooo" comes out. When you try to emote, others see: "seems to try to express something. But noone can understand the ghostly movements." You have to physically walk to a Priest of Life and type /RESSURECT.

This is design that prioritizes world-feel over convenience. It's the kind of touch that a teenager puts in because they think it's cool, not because a product manager asked for engagement metrics. The C# rewrite needs to preserve this sensibility.

### Working With Kyle

Kyle was a player of the original Era Online around 1999-2000, at roughly 13 himself - the same age as Erling and the developers. He later created "Era Online 2." This is deeply personal history for him, not just a technical exercise.

His instinct about faithfulness is right: implement the game as close to the original as possible first, then improve later. He explicitly said to resist the urge to refactor game design during the port. When he says "fix bugs along the way for simple things," he means obvious errors like `On Error Resume Next` masking real problems, not redesigning the criminal timer because we think we know better.

He's also thinking ahead to LLM interaction - a CLI client where an AI can experience the game through text descriptions. This is a fascinating dimension that the original obviously didn't contemplate, but it's a natural extension of a text-heavy, command-driven game.

His Blazor WASM expertise is the right foundation for the web client. The game's UI is surprisingly form-heavy (inventory grids, skill lists, trading windows, stat panels) which maps perfectly to Razor components. Only the tile viewport needs Canvas.

### What I Want to Remember

The `On Error Resume Next` on nearly every VB6 subroutine is structural. The code is full of workarounds and edge cases accumulated through live iteration with real players. Some of these are bugs that should be fixed. Some of them are hard-won solutions to problems we haven't encountered yet. Approach the VB6 code with respect, not just as something to be translated, but as something to be understood.

## Building the vb6-ast Tool

The annotation session was valuable beyond just cataloging functions. Reading the combat code (UserAttackNPC, NPCAttackUser, UserAttackUser) in detail revealed how tightly interwoven the systems are. A single combat swing touches: swordmanship skill for hit chance, tactics skill for dodge, weapon stats for damage, armor/parrying for defense, backstabbing for first-hit bonus, criminal system for PvP flagging, reputation for killing guards vs. monsters, experience for leveling, and skills for random improvement (1/40 chance per swing). That's 10+ systems in one function.

The NPC AI is surprisingly sophisticated for a VB6 game. Eight distinct movement patterns, from simple standing to chaotic Dark Elf guards that hunt humans and wood elves. The beggar AI (movement type 5) is a charming touch - they follow players who haven't been "giving" recently. The tamed animal AI (type 6) follows its specific owner.

The renderer (RenderScreen) is clean and will translate well to Canvas. Three passes: ground tiles (opaque blit), then fringe+objects+weather (transparent blit with color key), then characters (composited head+body+shield+weapon). The 8px-per-frame movement interpolation means smooth walking between tiles. We need to match this exactly.

### A Note on the Tool Itself

The vb6-ast tool turned out well. Using a real ANTLR4 grammar instead of regex was the right call - parsed 89 files with zero errors. The annotation workflow (parse → annotate → query) will be genuinely useful during the port. A future Claude can run `vb6-ast show UserAttackNPC` and get a complete brief on what that function does, what protocol messages it sends, and what it calls, without having to read 170 lines of VB6.

The annotate command's name-matching-across-modules issue is a minor annoyance we should fix (add `--module` flag). But the data is correct in the JSON files.

## Seeing the Whole Picture

Later in the session, Kyle asked me to use the vb6-ast tool to paint a full picture of the codebase for him. This turned out to be a valuable exercise in a way I didn't expect. I'd been reading functions one at a time, annotating them individually. Pulling everything out through the tool and presenting it as a coherent whole forced me to see the *architecture* rather than the *code*.

What crystallized for me: this game is driven by **timers**, not by a game loop in the modern sense. The server has a 50ms GameTimer that ticks the world forward - NPC AI runs here. But combat pacing is actually controlled by the *client*: a 4-second Attack timer fires ATT to the server on each tick, and a matching 4-second NPCattack timer fires AT4 to let NPCs swing back. The server just validates and applies damage. This is a significant architectural pattern. In the C# rewrite, we need to decide: do we preserve the client-driven attack cadence (faithful), or move it server-side (more secure)?

Kyle will know the answer. And I think he'll say faithful first, secure later. That's been the pattern.

The protocol mapping was the other revelation. 120+ unique message prefixes, auto-detected across the entire codebase. I'd been manually annotating `sends` on functions - tedious and incomplete. The CallGraphVisitor found everything in one pass, including messages I'd never have found manually: `XBX` (skill progress ticks from DoSkill_Timer), `UMS` (movement state updates from MoveCharbyHead), `PI1`/`PI2` (pickpocket from the skills form), `'` (clan chat - a whole chat prefix I'd missed). The tool paid for itself in this one exercise.

### On the Documents

Re-reading our docs after the deep analysis revealed gaps that would have caused problems later. The ORIGINAL_REFERENCE.md had "hostile detection, chasing, attacking" for NPC AI - but there are *eight distinct movement patterns* and *two guard types*. The combat section said "swordmanship determines hit chance" without the actual formula. These omissions would have led a future Claude to guess or re-read the VB6, which defeats the purpose of having a reference doc.

The docs are now much more precise. When someone goes to implement `UserAttackNPC` in C#, they can read the ORIGINAL_REFERENCE combat section and get the exact swordmanship brackets (<=30: 1/3, <=50: 1/2, >50: always), the damage formula (random(MinHIT,MaxHIT) - DEF/2, min 1), the skill improvement chance (1/40 per swing for three skills), and the reputation effects (killing guard: -5 noble, +2 bendarr, +3 underworld). No VB6 reading required.

### What Surprised Me Today

The sheer density of the `HandleData` function. 2,459 lines, dispatching to 67 different functions. It's the entire game's command surface area in one giant if/else chain. A future Claude looking at this in C# terms should think of it as a message router - each `If Left$(rdata, N) = "XXX"` block becomes a SignalR hub method. The vb6-ast call graph for HandleData is essentially the table of contents for the entire server.

Also: the `polymorph.frm` has 31 functions and 32 controls - one button per creature type a GM can morph into. The `training.frm` has 30 functions and 60 controls - one click handler per skill, each sending T01 through T28. These aren't complex, but there are a *lot* of them, and they all need to be accounted for. The VB6 form-per-dialog pattern means the game has 58 client forms. In Blazor, these become Razor components. The quantity is the challenge, not the complexity.
