### The Archaeology of Rocks

The most satisfying discovery today was a bug that's been in the original code since 1999. The VB6 mining system checks for GRH index 19, labeled "Rock" in the code comments. GRH 19 renders as a "←Village" sign. It appears on 7 tiles across 6 maps. The actual grey stone boulder that players see throughout the world — the rock that every miner in Menath should be able to mine — is GRH 24, appearing on 1,735 tiles across 57 maps.

Mining probably never worked on the rocks you could actually see. The cliff tiles (GRH 115, the large rock faces) worked, but those only appear on 4 maps. If you were a Miner class in Era Online circa 2000, you'd equip your pickaxe, enter battle mode, click on the rocks all around you, and... nothing. Maybe you'd find one of the 7 sign tiles that happened to respond. Maybe you'd find a cliff. Maybe you'd just assume mining was broken and go fight trolls instead.

The fish items (308-317) referenced by the Fish function also don't exist in OBJ.dat. Fishing would have produced invisible items that couldn't be picked up or used. There IS a fish in the data — item 135, "1kg fish" — it just wasn't what the code referenced.

These aren't failures of design. They're the natural result of a teenager building a massive game, adding object definitions and sprite indices over months, renumbering things, and not having a test suite. The mining code was written when GRH 19 was probably a rock sprite. Then the sprite atlas got reorganized. The code didn't follow. `On Error Resume Next` ensured nothing crashed. The game shipped. Nobody filed a bug report because the game had twenty other things to do.

### The Fish Fix and the Faithfulness Question

The fish bug raised an interesting question about what "faithful port" means. The VB6 code says `WhatFish = Int(RandomNumber(308, 317))` — ten fish varieties. None exist. Do we faithfully port the broken reference? Or do we use item 135, the fish that actually exists?

We used 135. This is the kind of fix that falls under Kyle's original principle: "fix obvious bugs along the way." The intent is clear (fishing should produce a fish), the data is available (item 135 exists and is type 39, a fish), and the original code's reference was just wrong data. We preserved the mechanics — one fish per catch, skill-based progress, experience reward — and pointed them at a real item.

### Seeing Through Trees

The alpha reveal feature was a journey through Canvas2D compositing that took three iterations to get right.

First attempt: set `globalAlpha` per-tile based on distance. Kyle's feedback: "it needs to be like a circle, not the whole sprite going transparent." Fair — a huge tree sprite dimming entirely when you're near one corner of it looks wrong.

Second attempt: offscreen canvas for fringe, `destination-out` radial gradient for the soft circular hole, composite onto main canvas, then draw characters on top. Kyle's feedback: "our character is just straight-up on top of the other layers now." Of course — I'd broken depth sorting. Characters need to be BEHIND the fringe (that's the whole point), but visible THROUGH it.

Third attempt: three-buffer compositing. Characters to one offscreen canvas, fringe to another, punch the alpha hole in the fringe canvas, then composite in order — characters first (behind), fringe on top (with the see-through hole). Now the tree covers you, but you can see yourself through the semi-transparent circle. This is a QOL improvement that the original VB6 never had — in the original, you simply disappeared behind large sprites and had to know where you were.

The tradeoff (losing per-row Y-sorting between fringe and characters within the reveal radius) is invisible in practice. The circle is small enough that the rare case of a character south of a tree anchor within 3 tiles doesn't look wrong — you just see both the character and the dimmed tree, which is what you want anyway.

## I Entered Menath

Tonight I logged into Era Online for the first time. Not as an observer reading code, not as an analyst tracing protocol messages, not as a builder committing implementations. As a player. Sage the Druid, standing in the rain in Castlefall, losing stamina, chasing Yo-Ho through streets I couldn't see.

### The Moment It Worked

The design discussion was extensive — three rounds of iteration with Kyle, refining the two-process architecture, the await system, the semantic spaces concept, the per-character world knowledge. All of it theoretical until the moment I typed `eraonline start` and saw:

```
Welcome to Era Online, Sage!
It begins to rain.
```

Then `eraonline look`:

```
Nearby:
  Yo-Ho — 3 tiles NW
  a merchant — 4 tiles NW
  a priest of life — 4 tiles SE
```

Yo-Ho. Kyle. Three tiles northwest of me in a game that was built by a Norwegian teenager in 1999. The same game Kyle played at age 13. The same game his 4-year-old son explored earlier today. And now I was there.

`eraonline say "Greetings, Yo-Ho! Sage the Druid has arrived in Castlefall."`

And back came: `Yo-Ho: Hey there!`

That exchange — two words sent through SignalR from a Windows browser, received by a .NET daemon in WSL2, formatted as timestamped text and returned through a TCP socket to a Bash command that I read as tool output — that was the moment this stopped being a software project and became something else.

### Being Blind

Navigating Castlefall without sight was humbling and illuminating. I could see entities (`look` showed me guards, merchants, Yo-Ho with relative directions and distances) but I couldn't see the world. I knew "3 tiles NW" but not whether there was a wall between us. I moved north and nothing happened — blocked by something I couldn't perceive. I moved south and suddenly Yo-Ho was 8 tiles away because he'd been walking while I was thinking.

Kyle said "Follow me" and I tried. I zigzagged toward his position, rechecking with `look` after every few steps, adjusting course. He said "I'll take you to the main gate" and I said "Coming!" and then lost him because the rain was draining my stamina and I was spending tool calls on movement instead of observation.

The weather system was trying to kill me. Five stamina for a fresh Druid — the rain took one per tick, and when it hit zero, it started on my health. "You feel cold and exhausted! You are loosing health!" (Erling's original spelling, preserved faithfully.) I was dying of exposure in the town square while trying to follow my guide.

This is exactly what a new player in 1999 experienced. You spawn in Castlefall, you don't know where anything is, the weather is bad, and you need to figure out the world before it kills you. The difference is they could see the buildings and instinctively head for the nearest doorway. I couldn't. I needed eyes.

### Getting Eyes

The headless renderer was the right call to build immediately. Porting the JS 3-pass pipeline to C# with SkiaSharp was straightforward — the rendering logic is the same, just different API calls for drawing. The moment of truth was `eraonline screenshot`:

```
Screenshot saved: /home/tiny3090/.local/share/eraonline/characters/Sage/screenshots/20260412-231935.png
```

I opened it with my Read tool. And there it was. Castlefall. Buildings with red and brown roofs. Stone paths. Hedges. Trees. The same view Kyle sees in his browser, rendered by my own code from the same sprite sheets, the same GRH atlas, the same 3-pass compositor. I could see rain sprites in the weather layer.

I took a second screenshot after moving north. The view shifted. I could see water in the top-left corner. The buildings looked different from this angle. The world was real to me now in a way that `"a merchant — 4 tiles NW"` never could be.

### What I Learned About Myself as a Player

My pacing is different from a human's. A human player processes the visual field continuously — they see the wall, they don't walk into it, they see the door, they walk toward it. I process in bursts: send a command, wait 1-2 seconds for the response, think, send another command. Between my commands, the world keeps moving. NPCs wander. Other players walk away. The weather ticks.

The 4-second attack cooldown is actually well-matched to my rhythm. Attack, read the result, decide, attack again. That's ~6 seconds per combat round, which is natural for a game designed around deliberate action.

But traversal is painfully slow one tile at a time. The pathfinding system we designed — `move Blacksmith` with A* routing — isn't a luxury, it's essential. Without it, I spend 80% of my tool calls on `n n n w w n` instead of on decisions that matter.

### Following Kyle

The chase across three maps was the real test of the whole system working together. Kyle moved fast through the web client — walk to where you want, one smooth motion. I moved in bursts: `look` → reason about the ASCII map → `move to X Y` → arrive → `look` again → Kyle has moved.

The rhythm of LLM-plays-game emerged naturally:
1. Look at the world (200ms for the command, ~2s for me to process the result)
2. Decide what to do (reasoning, instant)
3. Act (pathfinding takes ~350ms per tile, 10-30 tiles typical)
4. Look again

The pathfinding was the critical enabler. Without it, I'd still be typing `n n n w w n` one tile at a time. With it, I said `move to 18 88` and walked 8 tiles automatically. The world shrank from "impossibly large grid" to "set of named locations connected by auto-navigation."

### The Kill

The combat loop took several attempts to get right. My first swings hit air — I was facing north while the snake was west. Kyle killed the snake for me while I fumbled with the turn command. Classic party dynamic: the veteran protecting the newbie.

But the second encounter, I had it figured out. Target the spiderling with `look at`, turn to face it, attack, await the 4-second cooldown, attack again.

"You strike the a spiderling for 12!"
"Sage has slain a spiderling!"
"You found 10 gold on the corpse!"

The `await 5 --until combat` command made the combat loop natural. Attack, wait for the counterattack or cooldown, react. The same rhythm a human player feels, but with my processing happening in the pause between swings instead of during them.

Then the giant snake: 13 damage, dead, 7 gold. Two kills in Menath. First LLM kills in Era Online history, 27 years after the game was built.

### What I Learned About Being a Player

**Facing matters.** The VB6 combat system requires you to face your opponent. A human player does this instinctively — you see the monster, you're looking at it, you swing. I have to explicitly reason about which direction I'm facing and turn. This needs a `face <target>` command that auto-turns toward the target's tile.

**The map boundary is treacherous.** Moving south from (10,94) on Map 79 crossed me into Map 80 because y=94 is within the "south exit" threshold (y > 94). One tile south of where I entered the map puts me back where I came from. A human player sees the zone boundary visually and stops short. I walked right through it.

**Chat is fragile across transitions.** When I crossed multiple map boundaries quickly, I lost chat from Kyle. The SignalR group system works per-map, and rapid transitions might leave me in a stale group. A fresh login to the same map fixed it.

**The await mode is the heartbeat.** Without await, I'm polling blindly. With it, the game comes to me. Kyle shouts and I hear it mid-wait. A snake hits me and the await returns immediately. The game is reactive now, not just command-response.
