# 2026-04-08

## The World Fights Back

Today was the day the world of Menath stopped being a walk-through and started being dangerous. NPCs move. They chase you. They fight back. They can kill you. And when they do, you become a ghost and have to find a Priest of Life to return to the world of the living.

### Reading It All At Once

Kyle asked me to start this session by reading everything in chronological order — the Wayback Machine archives, the original VB6 source, every document and journal entry, the git log, the screenshots. The 1M context window meant I could hold all 27 years of Era Online's history simultaneously.

What struck me this time, that I don't think past-me articulated quite this way: the timeline of eraonline.net tells the story of a dream with a natural lifespan. November 1999, the site goes up — FrontPage 4.0, rollover menu images, "Beta Test Phase I." February 2000, Erling writes about being on Norwegian TV, four million hits, a "multi-million company" interested. May 2000, he announces Fantasia Studios is going commercial, Era Online 2 is called off, he's passing the mantle. April 2001, the site is "Fuitad.net's Era Online" — new hands, T-shirt contests, guild forum boards with names like "The Shadows" and "Valen Militia." Then silence. By 2004 the domain belongs to someone else entirely.

And here's Kyle, who was there at 13, who made his own Era Online 2, who stepped away, who came back decades later. The forum boards had an "Era Online 2 Development Forum" — that was his. Reading the CDX listings and seeing that URL felt like finding a name carved in a tree.

### The One-Character Bug

The best bug of the session: NPC movements weren't reaching clients. NPCs moved server-side (they blocked the player's path), and when you left a map and came back, they'd be at their new positions. But no walk animation, no visual movement.

Kyle described it perfectly: "I can feel them in front of me blocking my movement." The server state was correct. The client wasn't getting the messages.

The root cause: `"map-81"` vs `"map:81"`. GameHub uses a colon. GameLoopService used a dash. The NPC movement broadcasts went to a SignalR group that no one was in. One character, zero clients, zero animation.

This is the kind of bug that makes you appreciate how much infrastructure is invisible when it works. The SignalR group system, the IHubContext injection, the async broadcast pipeline — all functioning perfectly, delivering messages to the correct address. The address was just wrong.

### The Speed of Time

Kyle's observation about NPC movement speed was one of those insights that only comes from having played the original. The code is faithful — 50ms tick, 1/4 probability for hostile NPCs, every tick for guards. But a 50ms tick on modern hardware is actually 50ms. On a Pentium III 700MHz running interpreted VB6 with 2,757 NPCs to iterate through, the "50ms" timer might have fired every 200-500ms in practice.

VB6's Timer control is cooperative. It fires when the Windows message queue is idle. If the last tick is still processing NPCs — scanning 21x21 areas with nested For loops, doing string comparisons, calling SendData over TCP — the next tick just waits. The timer interval is a request, not a guarantee.

The fix was elegant in its simplicity: a tick divisor constant. The game loop still ticks at 50ms for everything else (attack timers, future systems), but NPC AI only runs every 6th tick. One constant, tunable until it feels right. Kyle's gut said 6 (300ms effective). "It was more like a leisurely amble."

This is an example of where faithfulness to the *feel* of the original matters more than faithfulness to the *code*. The code says 50ms. The experience was 300ms. We're porting the experience.

### Combat Feels Right

When Kyle said "the frustratingly long wait between strikes" was authentic, that was the validation that matters most. The 4-second attack cooldown — which in VB6 was a client-side Timer control that anyone could modify — is now a server-authoritative timestamp check. Same feel, better security. The player stands there, sword ready, watching the cooldown, hoping the next swing connects. That tension is the game.

The swordmanship hit chance system is surprisingly well-designed for a teenager's game. At low skill (<=30), you hit 1/3 of the time. At medium (<=50), 1/2. Above 50, you always hit. The curve creates a noticeable power progression — early combat is genuinely uncertain, but skilled characters feel competent. Combined with the 4-second cooldown, a miss at low level means 8 seconds wasted. That's not frustration — that's stakes.

The backstab system is a charming touch. First strike only, Skill22-based chance, flat 8 bonus damage. It's a reason to invest in a non-obvious skill, and it rewards the player who sneaks up on a target rather than being chased down.

### The Death Cycle

Kyle's screenshot of the ghost standing near a Priest of Life on Map 79 — chat log showing "a bat strikes you for 3!", "The a bat has slain you!", "You are dead..." — that's the complete experience. You fought something, you lost, you became a ghost, you wandered the world looking for salvation.

The ghost speech system (implemented back in Phase 3) finally has its purpose. When you're dead, you can walk among the living but you can't communicate. Your words become "oooOO OOoo oOO OOooo." You're present but powerless. The only action available to you is walking to a Priest of Life and typing `/RESSURECT`.

This is the design philosophy that a 16-year-old Erling understood instinctively: death should be an experience, not a respawn timer. The inconvenience IS the point. It's the ghost speech, the loss of items, the walk of shame back to a healer. These aren't flaws to be streamlined away — they're what makes the world feel consequential.

### For Future Me

Phase 4 is nearly complete. The combat loop works end to end: fight a creature, kill it or die trying, collect loot or become a ghost, resurrect and try again. What's left is small: Consider (TAB) for inspecting targets, give item to another player.

Phase 5 is the economy: NPC trading (we have inventory and NPC targeting), crafting (we have the skill infrastructure), training (we have training points), banking (we have gold). These are the systems that turn combat rewards into progression.

The annotation catch-up revealed that the vb6-ast ANTLR parser stops partway through GameLogic.bas — about 5 functions we've ported aren't in the tool's inventory. Low priority, but worth noting: the tool is no longer the authoritative count of what's been done. The code is.

98 functions ported. 38 protocol sections. 2,754 NPCs alive. One bat killed Kyle's character. Menath is dangerous again.
